How the arguments fit together
==============================

The papers share local-to-global reasoning, but their geometric reductions
are different. A local algebraic inequality becomes a theorem only after the
written proof establishes that every actual case belongs to its domain.

Angle payments and finite forests
---------------------------------

The finite-volume paper and frozen compact arithmetic20 edition assign payments
to corners of two-faces, then compare the accumulated payment with global
incidence or curvature identities. Finite vertex links give spherical Coxeter
forests; ideal links require their own affine factors and curvature accounting.
Context states, chosen outputs, and priority rules prevent one geometric
feature from funding several obligations at once.

The arithmetic argument additionally uses the actual ground field, cyclic Gram
products, and positivity at nonphysical embeddings. Field and norm restrictions
apply at the stated dimension and configuration; they are not transferred to
unrelated faces or to lower-dimensional forests without proof.

Farthest-vertex component energies
----------------------------------

The general compact17 and compact arithmetic15 revisions fix one original
farthest vector. Its orthogonal components have additive squared energies.
Actual feasible positive points
give lower bounds for those components; positive dual references and paid-foot
corrections combine several inequalities for that same vector. Arithmeticity
restricts actual fields and integral cycles after this common geometric budget.

Finite white extensions, native angles, foreign components, zero-own contacts,
and tail configurations require complete case inventories. The final forest
classification exhausts the remaining rank budget. A component theorem with
arbitrary spectators differs from a whole-forest theorem. Neither follows
merely by assuming a facet is itself a Coxeter polytope.

Right-angled dimensions and four-dimensional structure
------------------------------------------------------

The sharp dimension paper first derives a projection/height relation. Its
general branch uses a spherical-code tensor inequality, including its strict
boundary case. Its ideal branch combines the named four-dimensional kissing
and facet-minimum/equality inputs with the regular ideal 24-cell normal model.

The ideal RA4 volume paper first proves its volume gap and equality recognition,
then applies cutting and gluing under its stated facet-type hypothesis. The
compact RA4 paper instead bounds facet capacity and propagates excess through
metric and graph cases. Its recognition results leave unrestricted volume
minimality open.

Finite algorithms are mathematical propositions
-----------------------------------------------

A proof-relevant algorithm needs all of the following:

#. A finite mathematical domain, including parameters, symmetries, and guards.
#. Exact operations and a termination argument.
#. Soundness and completeness for the proposition being checked.
#. The exact output, with its geometric interpretation established separately.

Some checks replay a saved certificate; others regenerate an explicitly bounded
domain. These are different scopes. Optimizers may suggest certificates, but
acceptance uses independent exact reasoning or checks. Hashes bind bytes;
they do not prove classification or geometric coverage.

The :download:`computational proof contract <../computational_proof_contract.md>`
sets out these obligations, while the per-paper maps identify their proof
homes. This structure also explains why conditional formal kernels can be
useful without claiming to formalize the entire geometry.

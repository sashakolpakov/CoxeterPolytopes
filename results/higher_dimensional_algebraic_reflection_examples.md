# Higher-dimensional algebraic reflection polytopes: explicit noncompact baselines

## Result

The divisor-lattice idea does not stop in dimension nine.  There are already
explicit algebraically occurring reflective lattices whose Coxeter chambers
are finite-volume noncompact polytopes in

\[
\mathbb H^{14},\qquad \mathbb H^{19},\qquad \mathbb H^{21}.
\]

They arise respectively from a hyperkähler fourfold of `K3^[2]` type, a
complex K3 surface, and a supersingular K3 surface in characteristic two.
Thus the algebro-geometric factory is demonstrably effective far above
dimension nine on the **noncompact** side.  What fails is not the supply of
high-dimensional reflection chambers, but compactness of the rational
Picard-lattice chamber.

The examples below are in Alexeev's classification and computation of
reflective even hyperbolic 2-elementary lattices:
[arXiv:2209.09110](https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.09110).

## Three explicit chambers

For an even hyperbolic lattice `L` of rank `r`, let `W_r(L)` be the group
generated by all integral reflections in negative roots.  If `W_r(L)` has
finite index in `O^+(L)`, its fundamental chamber is a finite-volume
hyperbolic Coxeter polytope in `H^(r-1)`.  Alexeev gives the complete simple
root matrices and maximal parabolic subdiagrams for the following cases.

| lattice `(r,a,delta)` | algebraic realization | polytope | facets | maximal parabolics |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `(15,9,1)` | `Pic(Hilb^2(X))`, with `NS(X)=(14,8,1)` | `H^14` | 98 = 84 short `(-2)` + 14 long `(-4)` | 2114, in 20 symmetry orbits |
| `(20,2,1)` | the unique complex K3 with this Picard lattice | `H^19` | 50 = 45 short `(-2)` + 5 long `(-4)` | 581, in 13 symmetry orbits |
| `(22,2,0)` | the Artin-invariant-one supersingular K3 in characteristic 2 | `H^21` | 210 = 42 short `(-2)` + 168 long `(-4)` | 1,095,990, in 18 symmetry orbits |

The maximal parabolic diagrams are cusp certificates, so these chambers are
not compact.  In the K3 cases the isotropic rays also have a direct
algebro-geometric meaning: they encode genus-one fibrations.  For `(20,2,1)`,
Alexeev identifies the 13 symmetry types with 13 types of elliptic pencils
with section.

The rank-22 case is extremal in a different direction: by Esselmann's rank
bound, rank 22 is the maximum possible rank of a reflective hyperbolic
lattice.  Hence the corresponding `H^21` chamber is a natural upper
calibration point for any divisor-lattice search for finite-volume examples.

## Exact matrix audit

Alexeev attaches every Coxeter Gram matrix to the arXiv source.  For the v4
attachment `all_cases_in_2el_paper.txt`, whose SHA-256 is

```text
4cdf39f61f01238a883129551c0181427e5821b7fed2fabfea436cbf3627f457
```

an independent exact audit gives:

| lattice | matrix size | exact rank | exact inertia, including kernel |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| `(15,9,1)` | 98 | 15 | `(1,14,83)` |
| `(20,2,1)` | 50 | 20 | `(1,19,30)` |
| `(22,2,0)` | 210 | 22 | `(1,21,188)` |

Here the order is `(positive, negative, zero)`, with roots taken to have
negative square.  The first `r` roots form a nonsingular principal block in
each case.  Exact leading-principal-minor elimination has `r-1` negative
pivots followed by one positive pivot; the final block determinants are,
respectively,

\[
512,\qquad -4,\qquad -4.
\]

This verifies the ambient hyperbolic dimensions without relying on floating
point eigenvalues.

## The geometric caveat: root chamber versus divisor cone

These examples are genuinely attached to algebraic varieties, but the words
"attached to" must not be silently strengthened to "equal to the nef cone."

For a K3 surface, the nef cone is cut out by effective `(-2)`-classes and its
Weyl group is `W_2(L)`.  Alexeev's smaller finite Coxeter diagrams are for the
larger group `W_r(L)`, generated here by both `(-2)`- and `(-4)`-reflections.
The long `(-4)` mirrors are integral lattice reflections, but they are not
automatically walls of effective divisors on the K3 surface.  Thus the
50-facet `H^19` and 210-facet `H^21` chambers are full Picard-lattice
reflection chambers, not assertions that the corresponding K3 nef cones have
those finite wall sets.

For a projective irreducible holomorphic symplectic variety, Markman's theorem
does provide a much closer bridge: a prime exceptional divisor induces a
monodromy reflection in its Beauville--Bogomolov class
([arXiv:0912.4981](https://arxiv.org/abs/0912.4981)).  Nevertheless, one must
still prove that each chosen lattice root is represented by an MBM or prime
exceptional class and that the selected root chamber is the movable or
Kahler chamber.  Alexeev's 98-root `(15,9,1)` diagram alone does not supply
that identification.

This gives three distinct levels of output:

1. an abstract integral reflective lattice;
2. a reflective lattice realized as `NS` or `Pic` of an algebraic variety;
3. a Coxeter chamber equal to a nef, movable, or Kahler cone of that variety.

The examples above establish level 2.  A strict divisor-cone factory must
certify level 3 root by root.

## What finite volume means algebraically

For a Picard lattice `L`, the positive cone carries the hyperbolic measure
defined by the intersection or Beauville--Bogomolov form.  Reflectivity is the
finite-index statement

\[
[O^+(L):W_r(L)]<\infty.
\]

This is the precise lattice-theoretic translation of finite covolume.  In
geometric settings Torelli and cone theorems relate the residual quotient to
automorphisms or birational automorphisms, while primitive isotropic rays
become genus-one fibrations or boundary degenerations.  The invariant measure
is therefore visible through the divisor algebra, but not through coarse
classical invariants such as Chern numbers alone: it is encoded in the full
intersection lattice, its effective/MBM roots, and the finite-index action.

## Consequence for the compact search

A full rational Picard lattice of rank at least five is isotropic over the
rationals by Meyer's theorem.  Hence a finite-volume arithmetic reflection
chamber in the whole positive cone has cusps; increasing Picard rank produces
more noncompact examples, not compact ones.

The correct compact design requirement is therefore stronger:

> the algebraic object must supply both a high-rank divisor lattice and a
> self-adjoint arithmetic endomorphism whose totally-real eigenspace is
> anisotropic at the distinguished embedding.

For complex K3 surfaces the Picard-rank bound caps a quadratic eigenslice at
`H^9`.  A complex OG10-type hyperkähler variety has `b2=24` (Rapagnetta,
[arXiv:math/0606409](https://arxiv.org/abs/math/0606409)) and potential Picard
rank 22, so it is the first natural complex geometric carrier for a quadratic
eigenslice in `H^10`.  The next inverse-design problem is not merely
to find a reflective rank-22 Picard lattice, but to find an OG10 Picard
lattice with the required self-adjoint order action and then identify its
MBM walls with an acute finite Coxeter system.

## Status

This note records and exactly audits existing examples; it does not claim a
new Coxeter polytope.  Its contribution to the search is a clean calibration:

- the algebraic factory already reaches `H^21` for noncompact chambers;
- the nef/movable-cone equality is an additional geometric theorem, not a
  consequence of reflectivity; and
- compactness requires an anisotropic arithmetic eigenslice, not simply a
  larger Picard group.

Every section still has a certified forbidden ridge elsewhere, so no Coxeter H20 example was found.

## A concrete ambient doubling repairs the old ridge

Let `P` be the original 210-facet Borcherds H21 chamber, with outward normals `r_i`. Write `s169` for reflection in the norm-four wall 169. The new ambient polytope is

```
D = P ∪ s169(P).
```

Its distinct boundary normals are the original and reflected normals other than the glued wall. After exact deduplication, there are **388**.

Convexity follows directly from the inequalities. For every `j≠169`,

```
s169(r_j) = r_j − 2(r_j,r169)/r169² · r169
```

is a nonnegative combination of `r_j` and `r169`, because the original chamber is acute. Thus every listed boundary plane supports both tiles. Conversely, any point satisfying all boundary inequalities lies in `P` if its pairing with `r169` is nonpositive; otherwise reflection in `r169` puts it in `P`. Hence their intersection is exactly the two-tile union. It is convex and finite-volume. Each boundary plane contains an original or reflected complete facet, so these are actual ambient facets.

For the previous exact normal

```
E = 2sqrt(2) r34 + (3−sqrt(2)) r46
    −sqrt(2) r204 + 2sqrt(2) r210,
```

the original section ridge 1/169 had squared cosine `27/52`. In the doubled chamber, its boundary planes become `r1` and `s169(r1)`. Their section angle is **exactly π/2**, and an exact timelike point certifies an actual ridge. This is a genuine removal of the old angle defect. Another actual forbidden ridge, between `r2` and `s169(r178)`, still rejects this control section.

The ambient reflection does not preserve the section plane. The two pieces are sections of two reflected ambient chambers, with different section planes when pulled back to `P`; this is not an intrinsic doubling of the old H20 section.

```

and define

```

     + sqrt(2)/4 · (r34−r46−r204+r210),

## Why finite spherical stars cannot repair the old twelve fixed planes

For a subset `J` of the original wall labels, let `W_J` be the subgroup generated by their reflections. If `W_J` is finite, its Gram matrix must be positive definite. The corresponding full reflection star is the union of the `W_J` copies of `P`.

An actual bad section ridge at old walls `a,b` forces

```
a ∈ J  or  b ∈ J.
```

Indeed, its certificate is strict on every original wall except `a,b`. If both are outside `J`, the ridge point is strictly inside the fundamental `J`-chamber. Every other `W_J` chamber is locally separated from it. The original bad ridge therefore survives in the full star and in any convex chamber subunion containing `P`.

This necessary covering condition conflicts with positive definiteness for all twelve fixed planes. Each final certificate contains only:

- actual forbidden-ridge clauses, with exact timelike points strict on all other original walls;
- nonelliptic-pair clauses, certified by nonpositive two-by-two Gram determinant;
- larger nonspherical-subset clauses, certified by an explicit nonzero rational vector of square zero;
- a complete Boolean propagation tree proving these clauses inconsistent.

Across the twelve final cores there are **56 actual-ridge clauses, 33 nonelliptic-pair clauses and 11 larger null-vector clauses**. Ten cases need only pair clauses; two need larger nonspherical subsets as well.

For example, the control section already has actual bad ridges

```
71/210,   81/169,   81/194.
```

If wall 81 belongs to `J`, positive definiteness excludes both 71 and 210, leaving their bad ridge uncovered. If 81 does not belong to `J`, both 169 and 194 are required, but their parallel-wall Gram is singular. Thus no finite spherical star can repair this fixed plane, even though the single doubling above repairs one selected ridge.

Numerical geometry proposed candidate ridges and a SAT solver selected small cores. **Twenty-seven proposals in the two difficult cases failed exact certification and were discarded.** The final obstruction uses only independently verified actual-ridge certificates; its Boolean replay imports no SAT solver. The conclusion does not rely on the numerical proposal counts or on a numerical nonexistence claim.

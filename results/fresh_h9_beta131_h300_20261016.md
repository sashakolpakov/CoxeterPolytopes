## Why the map preserves the full chamber

Let \(H=(6,0^8,1)\) be the Vinberg basepoint, and let \(v\) be either
checked corner with incident Gram matrix \(S\). The certificate verifies
the exact decomposition

\[
 H=a v+\sum_i b_i N_i,\qquad a>0,\quad b_i\le0.
\]

Indeed \(b=S^{-1}(H\cdot N_i)_i\), the inverse is nonnegative, and all
basepoint products are nonpositive. For any later simple root \(r\),
complete zero-sector enumeration gives \(H\cdot r<0\), while acuteness
gives \(N_i\cdot r\le0\). Hence

\[
 H\cdot r=a(v\cdot r)+\sum_i b_i(N_i\cdot r)
 \ge a(v\cdot r),
\]

so \(v\cdot r<0\). No later simple wall can cut or touch either corner.
Their nine checked outward normals are therefore their complete incident
sectors in the full integral reflection chamber \(C\).

An integral lattice isometry normalizes the group generated by all
integral reflections, so \(T(C)\) is another chamber of the same reflection
arrangement. The verified positive map of complete outward sectors makes
\(T(C)\) and \(C\) coincide locally at the target finite corner; hence they
are the same chamber. Local finiteness here also follows from the two
primitive norm classes: a mirror meeting a fixed compact hyperbolic set
has bounded physical coordinates, while the conjugate positive-definite
form bounds its conjugate coordinates. Integrality then leaves finitely
many representatives.

The physical integral isometry group is discrete because its other real
embedding is positive definite. An infinite-order chamber stabilizer gives
infinitely many disjoint translates of a sufficiently small interior ball
inside \(C\). Thus \(C\) has infinite volume and the lattice is
nonreflective.

In particular, no finite-volume Coxeter polytope can have all wall
reflections preserving this literal lattice: its reflection group would
be a subgroup of the full integral reflection group, whose covolume is
infinite. Other lattices and arbitrary nonintegral geometric walls are
outside this conclusion.

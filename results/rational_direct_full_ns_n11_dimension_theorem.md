# A direct full-NS dimension theorem for rational Coxeter nef chambers

**Status (2026-08-10): proved, classification-free beyond the rational
surface MMP.**  Let \(S\) be a smooth projective rational surface.  Suppose
that its literal complete nef cone is a finite-volume Coxeter fundamental
chamber, its complete negative-extremal Mori alphabet is exactly the
smooth-rational \((-1),(-2),(-4)\) alphabet, every facet reflection is
integral on the full Neron--Severi lattice, and the facet group is
reflection-maximal.
Then

\[
 \boxed{\dim \mathbf P(\operatorname {Nef}(S))
       =\rho(S)-1\le 11.}                              \tag{0.1}
\]

Here \(\operatorname {Nef}(S)\) has full-dimensional ample interior, so its
projectivization has dimension \(\rho(S)-1\) even when the complete Mori or
nef cone has isotropic extreme rays.

This is an AG dimension bound, not a census and not an invocation of a
classification of reflective lattices.  The proof uses the rational-surface
MMP, relative ampleness, exceptional proximity, smooth-rational adjunction,
the elementary positive-root lemma for a crystallographic Coxeter system,
and an explicit spacelike mixed ray.  It does **not** cover an adapted
half-boundary host.

## 1. Exact hypotheses and theorem

Put

\[
                         L=\operatorname {NS}(S)
\]

with its integral intersection form of signature \((1,\rho(S)-1)\).  Assume:

1. the complete Mori cone \(\overline {NE}(S)\) is rational polyhedral and
   every **negative** extremal ray, equivalently every hyperbolic nef facet,
   is generated by a smooth rational curve of square \(-1,-2\), or \(-4\).
   Square-zero tangent Mori rays are allowed;
2. reflection in every literal nef facet preserves \(L\);
3. the projectivized complete nef cone is a finite-volume Coxeter
   fundamental chamber \(P\) for the group \(W\) generated by its facet
   reflections; and
4. \(W\) has no proper reflection overgroup in \(O^+(L)\).

Absolute discrete-reflection maximality, or maximality among arithmetic
finite-covolume reflection groups, implies hypothesis 4: \(O^+(L)\) is
discrete, and a finite-covolume subgroup has finite index in every discrete
overgroup.

Every irreducible negative curve spans an extremal Mori ray.  Consequently
hypothesis 1 applies to every such curve, not merely to a selected list.
It also supplies a literal exposed facet: if \(A\) is ample and \(C^2<0\),
then

\[
                  A_C=A+{A.C\over-C^2}C
\]

pairs to zero with \(C\) and strictly positively with every other
irreducible curve, since two distinct irreducible curves have nonnegative
intersection.  Rational polyhedrality then makes \(A_C\) nef and exposes
exactly the ray of \(C\).

### Theorem 1.1 (direct full-NS dimension bound)

Under hypotheses 1--4,

\[
                              \rho(S)-1\le11.           \tag{1.1}
\]

More precisely, choose a birational morphism from \(S\) to a minimal smooth
rational surface and let \(m\) be its number of point blowups.  The base and
the sharp conclusion forced by the proof are

\[
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
S_0&\rho(S)-1&\text{forced bound}\\ \hline
\mathbf P^2&m&m\le11,\\
\mathbb F_2&m+1&m\le10.
\end{array}                                             \tag{1.2}
\]

## 2. Minimal bases and one free exceptional chain

We first derive the marked birational frames rather than assume them.

### 2.1 The minimal base is \(\mathbf P^2\) or \(\mathbb F_2\)

The minimal rational surfaces are \(\mathbf P^2\) and \(\mathbb F_e\), with
\(e=0\) or \(e\ge2\).  On \(\mathbb F_0\), let \(c,d\) be the two ruling
classes.  The integral square-minus-two reflection in

\[
                              c-d                         \tag{2.1}
\]

swaps the rulings and fixes every total exceptional coordinate.  For any
sequence of blowups, a relative ample exceptional divisor \(D\) makes

\[
                         N\pi^*(c+d)+D                   \tag{2.2}
\]

ample for \(N\gg0\), and this divisor is orthogonal to \(c-d\).  Thus the
mirror (2.1) crosses the ample interior.  It is not a wall reflection of
\(W\), while adjoining it gives a proper reflection overgroup in \(O^+(L)\),
contrary to hypothesis 4.

On \(\mathbb F_e\), write \(s^2=-e\), \(f^2=0\), and \(s.f=1\).  If \(t\)
centres lie successively on the strict negative section, its final class is

\[
             \widetilde s=s-\sum_{i\in I}e_i,
             \qquad \widetilde s^2=-(e+t),
             \qquad f.\widetilde s=1.                  \tag{2.3}
\]

It is a primitive irreducible negative curve and hence a literal facet.
Its reflection sends

\[
                    f\longmapsto f+{2\over e+t}\widetilde s.           \tag{2.4}
\]

Full-NS integrality forces \(e+t\mid2\).  Therefore \(e>2\) is impossible,
and for \(e=2\) one has \(t=0\).  Hence the only bases are

\[
                       \mathbf P^2\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb F_2,     \tag{2.5}
\]

and every \(\mathbb F_2\) centre is off the negative section.

### 2.2 Relative ampleness forces width one

Use orthogonal total-transform coordinates

\[
 L=\pi^*\operatorname {NS}(S_0)
       \perp\bigoplus_{v\in V}\mathbf Z e_v,
 \qquad e_v^2=-1.                                      \tag{2.6}
\]

Suppose that two exceptional valuations \(i,j\) are incomparable.  Choose
positive proximity slacks \(\tau_v\) and solve backwards

\[
 \lambda_v=\tau_v+
       \sum_{w\text{ proximate to }v}\lambda_w.        \tag{2.7}
\]

The descendant sets of incomparable vertices are disjoint.  Varying
\(\tau_i,\tau_j\) therefore makes it possible to impose
\(\lambda_i=\lambda_j\) while keeping every slack positive.  For an ample
\(H\) on \(S_0\) and sufficiently small \(u>0\), the divisor

\[
 A_u=\pi^*H-u\sum_v\lambda_v e_v                       \tag{2.8}
\]

is ample by the relative Kleiman criterion, and

\[
                         A_u.(e_i-e_j)=0.               \tag{2.9}
\]

The root \(e_i-e_j\) has square \(-2\) and defines an integral reflection of
\(L\).  Its mirror crosses the ample interior, so it is not in \(W\); adjoining
it contradicts reflection maximality.  Thus every pair of exceptional
valuations is comparable: the exceptional forest has width one.

### 2.3 Facet integrality removes satellites

Width one says that each new centre lies either freely on the newest
exceptional component or at the intersection of that component with an
older exceptional component.  The second possibility is a satellite.

If an exceptional prime has \(k\) proximate children, its strict class and
square are

\[
             E=e_i-\sum_{j=1}^k e_{v_j},
             \qquad E^2=-(k+1).                        \tag{2.10}
\]

A satellite gives some older prime at least two children.  On the final
surface:

- \(k=2\) gives square \(-3\), outside the physical alphabet;
- \(k=3\) gives a primitive square-minus-four class.  Since the rational
  lattice \(L\) is unimodular, some \(x\in L\) has \(x.E=1\), and
  \(s_E(x)=x+(x.E)E/2\notin L\), contrary to facet integrality;
- \(k\ge4\) again lies outside the physical alphabet.

Hence there are no satellites.  Every successor is a free infinitely-near
point on the newest exceptional component, and the complete exceptional
prime list is

\[
                 e_i-e_{i+1}\quad(1\le i<m),
                 \qquad e_m.                            \tag{2.11}
\]

In particular, multiplicities of every nonexceptional irreducible curve are
nonincreasing along the chain.

## 3. Reflection maximality forces the missing line or fibre wall

The key global step is valid even when \(P\) is nonsimplicial.

### 3.1 Primitive facet roots

For a physical facet curve \(C\), orient the wall root toward the effective
side.  If \(C^2=-1\) or \(-2\), its class is primitive and we put \(r_C=C\).
If \(C^2=-4\), facet integrality and unimodularity force

\[
                              C=2r_C,
                 \qquad r_C\in L,\qquad r_C^2=-1.      \tag{3.1}
\]

Indeed, if \(C\) were primitive, a class pairing one with \(C\) would be
sent to a half-integral class by the reflection.  Thus every primitive facet
root has square \(-1\) or \(-2\).

For two distinct facets define

\[
 a_{ij}={2(r_j.r_i)\over r_i^2}.                       \tag{3.2}
\]

The entries are integral because the facet reflections preserve \(L\), and
they are nonpositive because distinct irreducible curves have nonnegative
intersection.  An ample divisor pairs strictly positively with every
\(r_i\), so the roots are positively independent: no nonzero nonnegative
real combination of them is zero.

### 3.2 Nonsimplicial positive-root lemma

Let \(Q=\bigoplus_i\mathbf Z\varepsilon_i\) be the free root module and
let the simple reflection indexed by \(i\) act by

\[
             \sigma_i(\varepsilon_j)
                  =\varepsilon_j-a_{ij}\varepsilon_i.  \tag{3.3}
\]

The usual Coxeter root-sign lemma, proved by induction on gallery length,
says that every real root \(w\varepsilon_i\) has either all coefficients
nonnegative or all coefficients nonpositive.  Here this is an action of the
**same** Coxeter group \(W\), not merely a formal look-alike.  Because \(P\)
is a fundamental Coxeter chamber, its facet generators give the Coxeter
presentation of \(W\).  The matrix \((a_{ij})\) is a symmetrizable generalized
Cartan matrix: multiplication of row \(i\) by \(-r_i^2/2\) makes it symmetric,
and

\[
                  a_{ij}a_{ji}
     ={4(r_i.r_j)^2\over r_i^2r_j^2}
\]

has exactly the Coxeter value \(4\cos^2(\pi/m_{ij})\) when the two walls
meet, and is at least four when they do not.  Thus (3.3) obeys precisely the
braid relations of the geometric facet generators, and each word in the
free Cartan action is the same word in \(W\).  Equation (3.2) makes all root
coefficients integers.  The map

\[
                    Q\longrightarrow L,
                    \qquad\varepsilon_i\longmapsto r_i \tag{3.4}
\]

intertwines the reflection actions.  Linear dependencies among the actual
facet normals only create a kernel of (3.4); they do not alter the integral
one-sign expansion in the free module.  Positive independence ensures that
a positive formal root cannot map to zero.  This is the precise reason that
nonsimpliciality causes no gap.

Now set

\[
 \alpha_0=H-e_1-e_2-e_3\quad\text{over }\mathbf P^2,
 \qquad
 \delta_0=f-e_1-e_2\quad\text{over }\mathbb F_2.       \tag{3.5}
\]

Whenever the indicated exceptional coordinates exist, these are primitive
square-minus-two roots, so their reflections lie in \(O^+(L)\).  Reflection
maximality forces those reflections into \(W\).  A geometric hyperplane
reflection in \(W\) is conjugate to a facet reflection: choose a generic
point of its mirror, avoiding every codimension-two intersection in the
locally finite chamber tessellation.  It cannot lie in a chamber interior,
because that chamber and its reflected image would then have overlapping
interiors.  Hence it lies in the relative interior of a unique chamber wall,
which must be its own mirror; chamber transitivity supplies the conjugacy.

Therefore the primitive normal is the image, under the same word in (3.3)
and (3.4), of some primitive facet root.  Both that image and the root in
(3.5) are primitive integral normals to the same mirror, so they agree up to
sign.  The free-root sign lemma therefore gives an integral one-sign
simple-root expansion of \(\alpha_0\), respectively \(\delta_0\).  If the
indicated coordinates do not exist, then \(m<3\) over \(\mathbf P^2\) or
\(m<2\) over \(\mathbb F_2\), and the dimension bound is already immediate.

The sign is positive.  Over the plane use degree \(H.x\).  Over \(\mathbb F_2\)
use the ample degree \((s+3f).x\).  Every oriented facet root has nonnegative
degree, with degree zero exactly for exceptional facets, while each root in
(3.5) has degree one.  Consequently

\[
               \alpha_0=\sum_i c_i r_i
       \quad\text{or}\quad
               \delta_0=\sum_i c_i r_i,
       \qquad c_i\in\mathbf Z_{\ge0}.                  \tag{3.6}
\]

### 3.3 Degree one isolates one physical root

Apply the degree functional to (3.6).  Exactly one nonexceptional facet root
occurs, its coefficient and degree are both one, and all remaining roots are
exceptional.

Over \(\mathbf P^2\), write the isolated root as

\[
                              r=H-\sum_i n_i e_i.       \tag{3.7}
\]

If \(r\) is itself a physical \((-1)\)- or \((-2)\)-curve, adjunction and
\(r^2=-1,-2\), together with
\(n_1\ge n_2\ge\cdots\ge0\), give respectively

\[
                         r=H-e_1-e_2
        \quad\text{or}\quad
                         r=H-e_1-e_2-e_3=\alpha_0.      \tag{3.8}
\]

The remaining possibility would be a physical \((-4)\)-curve \(C=2r\).
Then \(r^2=-1\), while adjunction for \(C\) gives \(K_S.r=1\).  Hence

\[
                         \sum_i n_i^2=2,
             \qquad     \sum_i n_i=4,                  \tag{3.9}
\]

which has no nonnegative integral solution.  Thus a half-\((-4)\) root
cannot be the isolated plane root.

If the first class in (3.8) occurred, the exceptional remainder in (3.6)
would be

\[
                      \alpha_0-(H-e_1-e_2)=-e_3.       \tag{3.10}
\]

But the nonnegative cone generated by (2.11) does not contain \(-e_3\):
writing \(\sum a_i(e_i-e_{i+1})+a_me_m\), its coefficient recursion forces
\(a_1=a_2=0\) and then \(a_3=-1\).  Therefore the second class in (3.8) is
forced, with zero exceptional remainder.  The line root \(\alpha_0\) is an
actual physical facet.

Over \(\mathbb F_2\), pushing (3.6) down to the base shows that the isolated
root has base class exactly \(f\), not merely ample degree one.  Write

\[
                              r=f-\sum_i n_i e_i.       \tag{3.11}
\]

The physical \((-1)\)- and \((-2)\)-cases give

\[
                         r=f-e_1
        \quad\text{or}\quad
                         r=f-e_1-e_2=\delta_0.          \tag{3.12}
\]

For a half-\((-4)\) root, \(r^2=-1\) and \(K_S.r=1\) would instead give

\[
                         \sum_i n_i^2=1,
             \qquad     \sum_i n_i=3,                  \tag{3.13}
\]

again impossible.  The first class in (3.12) would leave the exceptional
remainder \(-e_2\), which is outside the nonnegative cone (2.11).  Hence
\(\delta_0\) itself is an actual physical facet.

We have now derived the exact marked frames:

- over \(\mathbf P^2\), the first three centres follow one line, the fourth
  and all later centres leave its strict transform, and every exceptional
  successor is free on the newest component;
- over \(\mathbb F_2\), the first centre lies on a fibre and the second is
  the **fibre-following free infinitely-near point** at the intersection of
  the strict fibre with \(E_1\).  Only \(E_1\) is exceptional there; this point is not satellite.  Every later centre leaves the strict fibre, all
  successors are free on the newest component, and all centres are off
  \(s\).

## 4. The first mixed ray gives the depth bound

For completeness, this section proves the needed mixed inequalities directly;
it does not import a separate marked-frame theorem.

### 4.1 Spacelike wall-cone lemma

Orient every physical facet normal \(r_i\) so that

\[
                  \mathcal C(P)=\{x:x.r_i\ge0\ \forall i\}             \tag{4.1}
\]

is the homogeneous cone over \(P\).  A finite-volume chamber cannot have a
spacelike vector \(w\), with \(w^2<0\), in \(\mathcal C(P)\).  Indeed, if
\(h\) is an interior timelike vector, the ray \(h+tw\) first reaches the
future null cone at some \(t>0\).  Since
\(\operatorname {int}\mathcal C(P)+\mathcal C(P)\subset
\operatorname {int}\mathcal C(P)\), that null direction is interior to the
wall cone.  The chamber then contains an open ideal-boundary patch, which has
infinite hyperbolic volume.

### 4.2 Plane mixed ray

Assume \(m\ge12\) and put

\[
 w_{\mathbf P^2}=7H-3e_1-2e_2-\cdots-2e_{12}.          \tag{4.2}
\]

Then

\[
 w_{\mathbf P^2}^2=-4,
 \qquad K_S.w_{\mathbf P^2}=4.                         \tag{4.3}
\]

Its pairing is zero with the line root \(\alpha_0\) and with every standard
exceptional wall except

\[
       w_{\mathbf P^2}.(e_1-e_2)=1,
       \qquad w_{\mathbf P^2}.(e_{12}-e_{13})=2,        \tag{4.4}
\]

where the second wall means \(e_{12}\) when \(m=12\).

Let a hidden physical curve be

\[
 C=dH-\sum_i\mu_i e_i,
 \qquad \mu_1\ge\mu_2\ge\cdots\ge0,
 \qquad q=-C^2\in\{1,2,4\}.                           \tag{4.5}
\]

Distinct-curve nonintersection with \(\alpha_0\) gives
\(d\ge\mu_1+\mu_2+\mu_3\).  Adjunction gives

\[
                         \sum_i\mu_i=3d+q-2.           \tag{4.6}
\]

With \(T=\sum_{i>12}\mu_i\), direct substitution yields

\[
                 C.w_{\mathbf P^2}
                   =d-\mu_1-2q+4+2T.                  \tag{4.7}
\]

This is nonnegative for \(q=1,2\).  For \(q=4\), put
\(R=d-\mu_1\).  Then

\[
             R\ge\mu_2+\mu_3,
             \qquad C.w_{\mathbf P^2}=R-4+2T.          \tag{4.8}
\]

If \(T\ge2\), the result is immediate; if \(T=1\), monotonicity gives
\(\mu_2,\mu_3\ge1\), hence \(R\ge2\).  If \(T=0\), square and adjunction
give

\[
 \sum_{i=2}^{12}\mu_i=2d+R+2,
 \qquad
 \sum_{i=2}^{12}\mu_i^2=2dR-R^2+4.                   \tag{4.9}
\]

A negative pairing would require \(R=0,1,2,3\).  For \(R=0,1\), the bound
\(\mu_2+\mu_3\le R\) makes the first sum in (4.9) impossible.  For \(R=2\),
the only maximal branch has \(d=3\) and ten ones, whose square sum is ten
rather than twelve.  For \(R=3\), the eleven entries have total at most
twelve, whereas (4.9), together with \(\mu_1=d-3\), excludes \(d=3,4\) and
is already too large for \(d\ge5\).  Thus every physical wall pairs
nonnegatively with the spacelike vector (4.2), contradicting finite volume.
Therefore

\[
                              m\le11.                   \tag{4.10}
\]

### 4.3 Ruled mixed ray

Assume \(m\ge11\) and put

\[
 w_{\mathbb F_2}=4s+9f-2e_1-\cdots-2e_{11}.            \tag{4.11}
\]

Then

\[
 w_{\mathbb F_2}^2=-4,
 \qquad K_S.w_{\mathbb F_2}=4.                         \tag{4.12}
\]

It pairs one with \(s\), two with the eleventh exceptional wall, and zero
with \(\delta_0\) and every other standard wall.  Write a hidden physical
curve as

\[
 C=as+bf-\sum_i\mu_i e_i,
 \qquad \mu_1\ge\mu_2\ge\cdots\ge0.                  \tag{4.13}
\]

Nonintersection with the fibre wall and the negative section gives

\[
                       a\ge\mu_1+\mu_2,
                       \qquad b\ge2a.                  \tag{4.14}
\]

Adjunction and substitution, with \(q=-C^2\) and
\(T=\sum_{i>11}\mu_i\), give

\[
 \sum_i\mu_i=2b+q-2,
 \qquad
 C.w_{\mathbb F_2}=a-2q+4+2T.                         \tag{4.15}
\]

The pairing is nonnegative for \(q=1,2\).  For \(q=4\), it is
\(a-4+2T\).  The cases \(T\ge2\) and \(T=1\) follow immediately from
(4.14).  If \(T=0\), then

\[
 \sum_{i=1}^{11}\mu_i=2b+2,
 \qquad
 \sum_{i=1}^{11}\mu_i^2=-2a^2+2ab+4.                 \tag{4.16}
\]

A negative pairing requires \(a=0,1,2,3\).  The first two values contradict
the total sum and \(b\ge2a\).  For \(a=2\), only \(b=4\) can meet the sum
bound; ten ones have square sum ten instead of the required twelve.  For
\(a=3\), (4.14) bounds the total multiplicity by twelve, while \(b\ge6\)
requires at least fourteen.  Thus (4.11) also lies in every physical wall
halfspace, contradicting finite volume.  Hence

\[
                              m\le10.                   \tag{4.17}
\]

Equations (4.10) and (4.17), with the ranks in (1.2), prove Theorem 1.1.
\(\square\)

## 5. Scope and replay boundary

The theorem is strictly about the direct full Neron--Severi lattice.  Its
proof uses unimodularity twice: to reject a primitive physical
square-minus-four facet and to make all facet roots integral primitive
classes.  An adapted exponent-two host can change both the missing-mirror
test and the available half-roots, so no adapted-host conclusion is made.

The complete smooth-rational \((-1),(-2),(-4)\) **negative-extremal**
alphabet and rational polyhedrality of the full Mori cone are also literal
hypotheses.  Square-zero tangent rays are allowed because they do not cut a
hyperbolic facet.  The theorem neither replaces the negative alphabet with a
finite sampled wall list nor claims a bound when other negative-curve species
occur.

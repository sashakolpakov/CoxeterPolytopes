\chapter{Arithmetic dimension bounds and an effective high-dimensional
search}\label{arithmetic-dimension-bounds-and-an-effective-high-dimensional-search}

\textbf{Date:} 2026-08-12\\
\textbf{Status:} provenance and reduction theorem; no new numerical
nonexistence claim is made.

\textbf{Search-direction recheck, 2026-10-13.} Esselmann's original
publisher abstract gives the maximal reflective integral Lorentzian
lattice rank as 22, meaning hyperbolic dimension 21. The compact
arithmetic case is a different category and remains subject to Vinberg's
bound 29. The precise noncompact range, including the exclusion of
dimension 20, is restated in
\href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/1506.03111}{Belolipetsky, Section 1}, with
Borcherds' 21-dimensional example described in Section 7. These are
search restrictions, not new results of this repository. The current H22
geometric branch is nonarithmetic if it ever achieves finite volume; its
present volume is infinite. No rational-lattice search in the excluded
dimensions is warranted.

\textbf{Current-frontier cross-check.} The introduction of
Guo--Ma--Zang--Zheng's \href{https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.14715}{July 2026
preprint}, \emph{Several families of incommensurable noncompact
hyperbolic Coxeter polytopes}, still records known compact examples only
through dimension 8 and noncompact examples through dimension 21. Its
new constructions are families in dimensions 4, 5, 6, 7 and 9; they do
not raise either dimension record. This supplies a recent primary-source
check rather than relying only on the 2015 survey for the state of known
examples.

\textbf{Dimension 20 is also a target.} The known dimensions are not
simply every dimension up to 21.
\href{https://www.maths.dur.ac.uk/users/anna.felikson/Polytopes/polytopes.html}{Felikson's
research overview} lists finite-volume examples in dimensions at most 19
and in dimension 21, leaving 20 absent. Thus a nonarithmetic
finite-volume H20 example would also fill a new dimension in the sources
checked, although it would not raise the maximum-dimension record.
Esselmann's arithmetic exclusion in dimension 20 does not prove
unrestricted nonexistence there. The existing geometric H19-to-H20 lane
should be judged by its specific construction obstructions, not
discarded under the arithmetic theorem.

Consequently the displayed \texttt{n\textless{}=995} in the current
AGCat composite theorem is not the best arithmetic bound. It is the
bound one gets while importing only a theorem about \textbf{all}
finite-volume Coxeter polytopes. If Vinberg's arithmetic dimension
theorem is admitted, \texttt{995} can immediately be replaced by
\texttt{29}. If the chamber is noncompact, Esselmann improves this
further to the disconnected range \texttt{n\textless{}=19} or
\texttt{n=21}.

The compact and noncompact arithmetic cases behave differently. For a
noncompact arithmetic chamber a cusp supplies a nonzero isotropic vector
over the ground field. At every nonidentity real embedding the
admissible form is definite, so the ground field must be \texttt{Q}.
After scaling, one obtains a reflective integral Lorentzian lattice, and
Esselmann applies. This argument does not apply to compact chambers: in
dimensions at least four they cannot be defined over \texttt{Q}, since
the rational Lorentzian form would have at least five variables and
hence be isotropic by Meyer. For a genuine totally real ground field,
the nonidentity definite embeddings defeat the rational-isotropy
argument.

The strongest safe statement about the unknown compact range is
therefore:

\begin{quote}
No compact hyperbolic Coxeter polytope is presently known in dimension
at least nine, arithmetic or otherwise, and for each dimension
\texttt{9,...,29} the unrestricted existence/nonexistence problem
remains unresolved. This is not a nonexistence theorem.
\end{quote}

There are many conditional or bounded-facet nonexistence results inside
this range, but they do not settle arbitrary compact Coxeter polytopes.

contribute}\label{what-the-agarrangement-theorem-does-and-does-not-contribute}

The exact carrier and field funnel make the classification problem more
discrete, but they do not presently prove a rank bound. Their
contribution is

\section{3. A rigorous finite reduction for dimensions 22 through
29}\label{a-rigorous-finite-reduction-for-dimensions-22-through-29}

Vinberg's refined high-dimensional theorem makes the first target much
smaller than the global field theorem alone suggests. For an arithmetic
reflection group in dimension \texttt{n\textgreater{}=22}, the complete
high-dimensional ground-field list is

\[
 \mathbf Q,\qquad \mathbf Q(\sqrt2),\qquad
 \mathbf Q(\sqrt5),\qquad \mathbf Q(\cos(2\pi/7)).       \tag{3.1}
\]

The rational field cannot occur in the \textbf{compact} case here.
Arithmetic reflection groups are of simplest type; in dimension at least
four their defining rational Lorentzian form has at least five variables
and is isotropic by Meyer, so the corresponding arithmetic lattice is
noncocompact. Thus a compact arithmetic reflection group with
\texttt{n\textgreater{}=22} has ground field in precisely the
three-field candidate list

\[
 \mathbf Q(\sqrt2),\qquad
 \mathbf Q(\sqrt5),\qquad
 \mathbf Q(\cos(2\pi/7)).                              \tag{3.2}
\]

Fix one of these fields and \texttt{22\textless{}=n\textless{}=29}, and
put \texttt{m=n+1}. Pass from any finite-covolume arithmetic reflection
subgroup to its full integral reflection host. Compactness is preserved
under this finite-index enlargement. The bounded connected-frame lemma
supplies \texttt{m} chamber walls spanning the Lorentzian space and
satisfying, for every frame edge,

\[
 c_{ij}<62^2                                                   \tag{3.3}
\]

at the distinguished embedding. For every simple directed cycle
\texttt{C} of length \texttt{r}, including length-two cycles,

\[
 b_C\in\mathcal O_k,\qquad
 |b_C|<62^r,\qquad
 |\sigma(b_C)|\le 2^r\quad(\sigma\ne\sigma_0).          \tag{3.4}
\]

The Minkowski embedding therefore gives an \textbf{explicit finite box}
of possible values for every \texttt{b\_C}. There are finitely many
connected graphs on \texttt{m} vertices. Equality of all simple cycle
products determines the ordered reflection representation up to diagonal
gauge.

There is a small effectivity point here which must not be hidden behind
an assumed invariant lattice. It can be resolved directly from the same
cycle data. Write \texttt{T\_i=1-s\_i}. The trace of a product
\texttt{T\_\{i\_1\}\textbackslash{}cdots\ T\_\{i\_r\}} is the product
along its closed directed walk. If it is nonzero, that walk decomposes
into simple directed cycles (and loops, for which \texttt{a\_ii=2}), so
its trace is a product of integral \texttt{b\_C} and powers of
\texttt{2}. Expanding words in the \texttt{s\_i} therefore shows

\[
             \operatorname {tr}(w)\in\mathcal O_k
             \quad\hbox{for every word }w\hbox{ in the }s_i.       \tag{3.5}
\]

Burnside's theorem says that the word matrices span
\texttt{A=End\_k(V)}. Enumerate words until \texttt{m\^{}2} of them,
\texttt{e\_1,\textbackslash{}ldots,e\_\{m\^{}2\}}, form a
\texttt{k}-basis, and compute the trace-dual lattice

\[
 D=\{x\in A:\operatorname {tr}(xe_j)\in\mathcal O_k
                    \text{ for every }j\}.                         \tag{3.6}
\]

The trace pairing on \texttt{A} is nondegenerate, so \texttt{D} is a
computable full \texttt{O\_k}-lattice. Equation (3.5) gives

\[
 \sum_j\mathcal O_k e_j
       \ \subseteq\ \Lambda:=\mathcal O_k[s_1,\ldots,s_m]
       \ \subseteq\ D.                                             \tag{3.7}
\]

In particular, \texttt{D/(sum\_j\ O\_k\ e\_j)} is finite. Starting with
the span of
\texttt{1,s\_1,\textbackslash{}ldots,s\_m,e\_1,\textbackslash{}ldots,e\_\{m\^{}2\}}
and repeatedly adjoining all left products by the \texttt{s\_i} computes
\texttt{Lambda}: the ascending chain stays inside \texttt{D} and must
stabilize in that finite quotient. Choose any full seed lattice
\texttt{L\_b} in \texttt{V} and set \texttt{L\_0=Lambda\ L\_b}; this is
now a \textbf{computed} full projective \texttt{Lambda}-stable lattice,
not an oracle input.

Put \texttt{B=End\_Ok(L\_0)}. The annihilator of the finite module
\texttt{B/Lambda} computes a nonzero conductor ideal \texttt{c} with
\texttt{cB\ subset\ Lambda}. Standard exact number-field algorithms
compute ideal-class representatives, and the remaining lattices are
obtained by enumerating the submodules of the finite modules
\texttt{M/cM} that are stable under \texttt{Lambda}. Thus the full-order
conductor sandwich really gives a finite algorithm for the stable
projective lattices, rather than only a qualitative finiteness
statement.

Finally, the effective termination theorem of Belolipetsky--Kapovich
turns Vinberg's semi-algorithm into a decision procedure for each
resulting arithmetic lattice. Exact cone and vertex-link calculations
decide finite volume and compactness.

This proves the following reduction, but not its hoped-for verdict.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Finite high-dimensional decision reduction.} The existence and
nonexistence of compact arithmetic reflection groups in dimensions
\texttt{22,...,29}, and the classification of the resulting full
integral reflection hosts (which suffices for the reflection-maximal
chambers in this reduction), reduce to a finite exact enumeration over
the three fields (3.2). A complete empty enumeration would improve the
dimension theorem for all arithmetic reflection groups from
\texttt{n\textless{}=29} to the sharp numerical ceiling
\texttt{n\textless{}=21}.
\end{quote}

This does \textbf{not} assert a finite classification of all nonmaximal
reflection subgroups inside a surviving host. Such subgroups are not
what the host enumeration or the Belolipetsky--Kapovich termination
theorem classifies, and there can be infinite reflection-subgroup
constructions. They are irrelevant to the proposed nonexistence
decision: every arithmetic reflection subgroup has a full host, and
compactness survives the finite-index enlargement.

This reduction is stronger and more concrete than merely saying that the
global set is finite. It identifies eight ranks and three fields and
gives a certificate format. It is still likely enormous: at
\texttt{m=30} there are \texttt{2\^{}435} labelled support graphs before
structural pruning, and the bounds in (3.4) are deliberately crude.

For dimensions \texttt{14,...,21}, Vinberg similarly restricts the
compact ground field to a short explicit list of quadratic, biquadratic,
and real cyclotomic fields. Dimensions \texttt{9,...,13} can still be
made finite by the global field funnel, but its finite field list must
first be materialized rather than left as a Fekete finiteness assertion.

\section{4. Proof-quality computation and
certificates}\label{proof-quality-computation-and-certificates}

A credible implementation should enumerate constraints rather than raw
graphs.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Cycle/frame stage.} Incrementally enumerate connected Cartan
  supports modulo graph automorphisms and spanning-tree gauge. Enforce
  exact Coxeter values for intersecting walls, symmetrizability cycle
  identities, selected signature \texttt{(n,1)}, positive definiteness
  at the other embeddings, and the bounds (3.3)--(3.4). SAT/SMT may
  propose supports, but every accepted or rejected branch must be
  checked in exact number-field arithmetic.
\item
  \textbf{Arithmetic-host stage.} Construct the reflection order,
  enumerate its stable projective lattices through ideal classes and
  conductor submodules, and quotient by field, lattice, and diagram
  automorphisms.
\item
  \textbf{Vinberg stage.} Run the effective Vinberg algorithm for each
  host. Certify root ordering, completeness, exact Gram inertia, all
  finite and ideal vertex links, and absence of hyperideal extreme rays.
  Test compactness and deduplicate the resulting full mirror chambers.
\item
  \textbf{Coverage certificate.} Freeze a manifest for every branch with
  one exact rejection reason: non-Coxeter entry, failed symmetrization,
  wrong inertia, failed nonselected definiteness, no stable lattice,
  nonreflectivity, noncompactness, or duplication. A small independent
  verifier should replay the finite-box coverage and every terminal
  witness without floating-point tolerances.
\end{enumerate}

For finding examples, run the same pipeline in the opposite order:
prioritize small discriminant, small two-cycle values, sparse connected
supports, and small conductor. A survivor is then completed by Vinberg's
algorithm. This is much more plausible than scanning facet lists
directly.

\section{5. Where small ridges help, and where they do
not}\label{where-small-ridges-help-and-where-they-do-not}

Bogachev proves that a compact Coxeter polyhedron in \texttt{H\^{}3} has
a ridge of width less than \texttt{5.75}; the same bound holds in higher
dimension when the polytope has a three-dimensional face which is itself
Coxeter. This improves Nikulin's general width bound \texttt{7}
dramatically in the affected classifications (the paper reports, in its
rank-four examples, reductions from roughly \texttt{280} to at most
\texttt{85} candidates over \texttt{Q}, and from roughly \texttt{160} to
\texttt{83} over \texttt{Z{[}sqrt(2){]}}).

This is excellent pruning for low-rank classification and for any
higher-dimensional branch in which a Coxeter three-face has been
certified. It is not currently a universal dimension argument: a face of
a Coxeter polytope need not itself be a Coxeter polytope, and no theorem
used here forces the required three-face in every compact
high-dimensional chamber.

A promising way to obtain a \textbf{new numerical} bound is instead to
revisit Vinberg's weighted two-face proof. Its combinatorial proposition
assigns weights to planar angles and requires \textbf{both} a vertex
inequality (the total weight at every vertex is at most \texttt{cn})
and, for every \texttt{k}-gonal two-face, a face inequality (the total
weight around it is at least \texttt{5-k}). Under those global
hypotheses its conclusion has the form

\[
                         n<8c+6.                         \tag{5.1}
\]

Vinberg constructs a globally valid assignment with \texttt{c=3}, giving
\texttt{n\textless{}30}. Improving this is not merely the calculation of
a smaller local maximum. In the high-dimensional arithmetic range one
may enumerate candidate local configurations over the restricted fields
using the integral cycle coordinates, but one must then exhibit and
verify an assignment satisfying the vertex and face inequalities
simultaneously for every possible chamber. This can be organized as a
finite exact linear-programming/covering certificate only after
exhaustive coverage of the configurations has itself been proved. For
example, such a global certificate with \texttt{c\textless{}=23/8} gives
\texttt{n\textless{}29} and therefore rules out dimension \texttt{29}.
Each further decrease gives a rigorous incremental improvement. This
target is potentially far smaller than classifying every full chamber
while still retaining an auditable finite certificate.

The obstruction is precise: field finiteness and small ridge bounds
alone do not decrease \texttt{c}; one must prove exhaustive coverage of
all configurations that can occur and a globally valid weight assignment
satisfying both families of inequalities.

\section{Proof with arbitrary pendant
trees}\label{proof-with-arbitrary-pendant-trees}

Use unit-square normal vectors. The finite-contact alphabet over this
field is \(2,3,4,6,8\), as derived in the
\href{fresh_arithmetic_sqrt2_local_20261015.md}{frozen quadratic local
package} and its
\href{fresh_arithmetic_sqrt2_local_20261015_review.md}{independent
review}. A bridge or pendant bond belongs to an actual spherical corner
component of rank at least three. Finite labels 6 and 8 must be isolated
rank-two components, so every such bond here has label 3 or 4. In
particular its squared Gram entry is rational. This step is needed: a
label-8 square would otherwise introduce a new irrational term in the
bridge correction.

Schur-eliminate the pendant white trees. Their positive-definite Gram
matrices are forest matrices with rational squared bonds. Successive
leaf elimination therefore subtracts rational, nonnegative quantities
from the diagonal of the unique attachment node and creates no
additional edge. The resulting cycle diagonals \(p_i\) are positive
rational numbers at most one. Positivity follows from the original
positive-definite white support. Orthogonal white components can be
removed as positive factors.

For a cycle of order \(r\in\{3,4,5\}\), its reduced determinant has the
form

\[
\det L=a(p)-b_r\sqrt2,
\qquad b_r=2^{1-r}>0.
\]

The two oriented full-cycle terms are the only irrational determinant
terms; all matching terms use rational bond squares. Thus the
coefficient \(b_r\) is independent of the diagonals. Every proper
principal minor is a forest determinant and is rational. Direct
calculation gives

\[
a(1,\ldots,1)=0\quad(r=3),
\qquad a(1,\ldots,1)=-\frac1{16}\quad(r=4,5).
\]

At the other arithmetic embedding the full Gram is positive
semidefinite. This property survives Schur elimination of the white
trees. The conjugate cycle block is actually positive definite: its
determinant is \(a(p)+b_r\sqrt2\), which cannot vanish because \(a(p)\)
is rational and \(b_r\ne0\). Increasing the reduced diagonals to one
preserves positive definiteness. The derivative of the determinant with
respect to any diagonal is a positive proper principal cofactor, which
is rational and unchanged by conjugation. Consequently

\[
a(p)\le0\quad(r=3),
\qquad a(p)\le-\frac1{16}\quad(r=4,5).
\]

Write the two reduced cycle determinants as \(a-b\sqrt2\) and
\(A-B\sqrt2\). A single bridge of magnitude \(\delta\), joining nodes
\(u,v\), gives

\[
\det G=(a-b\sqrt2)(A-B\sqrt2)
-\delta^2\det(L-u)\det(M-v).
\]

The second term is rational. The coefficient of \(\sqrt2\) is therefore

\[
-aB-Ab>0,
\]

because at least one cycle has order four or five. Thus the reduced
determinant is nonzero, and restoring the eliminated positive-definite
factors does not change that conclusion.

But the \textbf{complete} star has \(n-2\) independent white nodes and
four black nodes in a Lorentz space of dimension \(n+1\). Its Gram
determinant must vanish. This contradicts the nonvanishing just proved.
Applying this rank count to a smaller subdiagram while omitting other
connected white nodes would be invalid; completeness of the specified
topology is an explicit hypothesis.

\section{A quadrilateral with no short marked
corner}\label{a-quadrilateral-with-no-short-marked-corner}

Let W be the spherical white support of an actual compact quadrilateral
two-face, and let a,b,c₀,d be its four black sides in cyclic order. The
symbol c₀ denotes a node; c continues to denote cos(π/7). Each W plus an
adjacent black pair is positive definite. The opposite extensions W+a+c₀
and W+b+d are indefinite. They cannot be positive definite by the face
correspondence, and cannot be affine in a compact chamber. Their minimal
indefinite subsets L,M include both respective black nodes; hence they
have order2 or3.

Assume no adjacent black pair is connected by a path of length at most2
in its finite corner diagram. The following reduction is exhaustive.

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  All adjacent black pairs are right. A Lannér2 opposite pair would be
  orthogonal to the other minimal indefinite diagram: any cross black
  bond gives a length-one marked path, while a cross black-to-white bond
  gives a length-two marked path. Two orthogonal indefinite diagrams
  violate Lorentz index one. Thus both L and M are triangles.
\item
  Their white nodes u,v are distinct. Their only possible connecting
  bond is u--v. A high bond involving a white node would be isolated in
  each finite corner and would force u--v right, again making L and M
  orthogonal. Therefore the high bonds are the two opposite black pairs
  a--c₀ and b--d, and u--v is nonright.
\end{enumerate}

The checker enumerates every binary support of the two connected
diagrams, all cross-bond subsets, shared or separate white nodes, and
all choices of distinguished high bond. It only imposes the necessary
finite-corner forest/isolation conditions and absence of a short marked
path. The nine remaining supports are precisely the nonempty attachment
patterns on either side of the two high opposite bonds, joined by u--v.
This is a relaxed complete finite verification, not a numerical Gram
test.

\subsection{Eliminate label14 and dense

\[

positive entries. If either opposite bond is14, or either white node
touches both endpoints of its opposite7 bond, the two residual white
diagonals are less than−4 and−6/19. Their off-diagonal remains their old

block. Its square is at most1. Thus their determinant exceeds

\[
 4\frac6{19}-1=\frac5{19}>0.
\]

This supplies a negative-definite two-plane, impossible in a Lorentz
Gram. Consequently both opposite bonds are7, and every white neighbor
touches exactly one endpoint of its associated opposite pair.

neighbors of b or d.~They are nonempty and disjoint. Each triple

\section{Strict dimension inequality}\label{strict-dimension-inequality}

For a simple compact n-polytope, let \(h_0,\ldots,h_n\) be the symmetric
nonnegative h-vector of its simplicial dual. Then \[
f_0=\sum h_i,\qquad
f_2=\sum_i\binom{n-i}{2}h_i.
\] After symmetry, the coefficient of \(h_i\) in \(f_2\) is
\(\{\binom i2+\binom{n-i}2\}/2\). Its minimum is \(n(n-2)/8\) for even n
and \((n-1)^2/8\) for odd n.~Since \(h_0=h_n=1\) and the two endpoint
coefficients are strictly larger than the minimum, \(f_2/f_0\) is
\textbf{strictly} larger than this minimum.

Conditional on quadrilateral weight at least 1, every k-gonal two-face
has weight at least \(5-k\): triangles and quadrilaterals give the
nontrivial cases, while nonnegative weights suffice for \(k\ge5\).

\frac{\text{total vertex weight}}{f_0}
\ge5\frac{f_2}{f_0}-\binom n2
>
\begin{cases}n(n-6)/8,&n\text{ even},\\
(n-1)(n-5)/8,&n\text{ odd}.
\end{cases}
\] For \(n=21+2u\) and \(n=22+2u\), subtraction of \(2n-2\) gives
respectively \(u^2/2+5u\) and \(u^2/2+11u/2+2\). Both are nonnegative
for every integer \(u\ge0\). The first is zero at n=21, where the strict
h-vector inequality still contradicts the vertex upper bound 40. Thus
the conditional conclusion is n at most 20, with no parity or equality
gap.
